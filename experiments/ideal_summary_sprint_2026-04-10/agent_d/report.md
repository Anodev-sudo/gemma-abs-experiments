# Agent D Report

## Main thesis

The ideal summary for this project is not just short and punchy. It should answer one question fast: why might someone open this paper? On a phone, that usually means naming the concrete object of study and the sharpest payoff, then stopping before the line turns into an abstract fragment or a slogan.

## What an ideal summary should do

- Stay to one sentence and usually land around 10-14 words.
- Add value beyond the title, not just paraphrase it.
- Prefer the strongest result, capability, limit, or implication over setup.
- Sound natural when read alone in a scrolling feed.
- Keep method words only when the method itself is the interesting part.
- Be willing to stay a little literal for math or theory papers instead of forcing a fake consumer-headline tone.

## What the current pipeline gets right

- It is correctly obsessed with brevity.
- It pushes against filler openings and title restatement.
- It is aiming at the actual product surface rather than generic summarization.
- It has moved in a good direction by preferring payoff-first lines over abstract-style paraphrases.

## What the current pipeline still gets wrong

- It sometimes treats "headline-style" as a license to sound glossier than the paper warrants.
- The anti-article opening rules are useful, but taken too literally they can produce strained phrasing.
- Some papers, especially math, statistics, and survey-like work, need calm specificity more than boldness.
- The reviewer baseline is good at rejecting obvious misses, but it does not define an ideal summary as clearly as it defines a reject.

## What I learned from the 10-paper exercise

- The hardest cases were broad review or framework papers, because the abstract often explains importance better than it states one crisp payoff.
- XR, audio generation, and materials papers were easier because they exposed a clean comparative result or behavior change.
- Theory papers still work in a feed if the line names the object and the gain, even when the gain is mainly "now has a closed form" or "connects two hard ideas."
- The best summaries here often sound slightly less aggressive than the current generator prompt, but more trustworthy.
